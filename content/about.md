Hi there!
This is Kiarash Azarnia. I'm a software engineer with about a decade of experience *(at the time of writing this note)*, and if there is a single thread running through everything I do, it's this: **I like to go to the roots of concepts and problems.** Not just fix what is broken, I really enjoy understanding why it broke, what shaped it, and what the system around it is really doing. This is the story of how that mindset formed.

## The early years: falling in love with the craft

I started out as a Java developer building enterprise applications. Those first three years were shaped by books as much as by code. I was delighted by Martin Fowler's writing — *Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture* above all, along with the *Fundamentals of Software Architecture* by Mark Richards and Neal Ford, and Eric Evans' *Domain-Driven Design*. And then there was Joshua Bloch's *Effective Java*, a book I genuinely loved and actually used, day after day.

Somewhere in those years I met the **Actor Model Programming** through Scala and Akka, and with it my first real performance challenge: contributing to a big refactoring; migrating a legacy messaging core to Akka and Kafka so the platform could scale to potentially multi-million users. That project was an opportunity for me into designing and building a distributed load-testing platform on Kubernetes and the ELK stack, and I was hooked.

## The cloud years: the system is more than the code! Much More!

Then I joined a private cloud provider and stayed for almost four years, long journey! ha, spanning multiple teams and products, taking on diverse responsibilities as an IC engineer: building software, driving technical initiatives, mentoring, improving processes, analyzing products, and providing on-call support. Few experiences teach you as much as being trusted with different lenses on the same organization. That best opportunity helped me understand why [being glue](https://www.noidea.dog/glue) is important for the success of a team.

My testing instinct followed me into security. On the access management (IAM) system, I wrote model-checking-style tests, mutation and fuzz testing, that surfaced several real bugs and meaningfully hardened the platform. I keep returning to software verification from time to time. I haven't been able to give it my full focus yet, but I hope to eventually.

Then I fell in love with **Kubernetes**. I read everything I could about the container wars and their main actors, an era full of business lessons: conflicts of interest, and how profoundly business shapes technical decisions! I came to admire the way Kubernetes is governed as the leading standard for cloud platforms. I contribute to the project both out of technical interest and to experience the reverse Conway maneuver firsthand.

> If you want to build a distributed computing platform, Conway's law suggests that you need a distributed decision-making setup.
>
> Sara Novotny - [Kubernetes: The Documentary - Part 2](https://youtu.be/318elIq37PE?si=7E3BZ82GV6VsPz-6&t=1155)

I still love this product and the way it is being managed. My shelf from that era includes *Kubernetes: Up and Running*, co-authored by Kelsey Hightower, with chapters written by pioneers of the project, especially the Network chapter by Tim Hockin, and Mark Burgess' *Promise Theory*, whose mathematical backbone was really fun for me.

Just as instructive has been watching how that large open-source organization actually works: the recent retirement of the [Ingress NGINX controller](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2025/11/11/ingress-nginx-retirement/), also the new [AI Policy](https://www.kubernetes.dev/docs/guide/pull-requests/#ai-guidance) the team had released, the foundation's response to AI-generated code, every decision is a live case study of a really complex socio-technical system.

The reading of those years also created my tendency to see a software engineer as a **Knowledge Worker**: Will Larson's *An Elegant Puzzle*, the *Google SRE Book*, DORA's *State of DevOps* reports, and the writing of Tanya Reilly and Charity Majors.

## Experiencing the scale!

Then I was given the opportunity to join a scaling wealth-tech startup as interim CTO. One of the co-founders was a former teammate who deeply trusted my work, which made for a great fit. During my six months there, I led an intensive growth phase: establishing the initial [engineering strategy](https://staffeng.com/guides/engineering-strategy/), hiring technical leads, revising team topology and OKRs, and defining first-generation career ladders, evaluation processes, and incident response protocols. What a ride! I can't overstate how intense and informative that period was, culminating in the hiring of a long-term CTO and the stabilization of our new teams and leaders.

## Nowadays

It's been almost a year that I work as a site reliability engineer again trying [the Engineer/Manager Pendulum](https://charity.wtf/p/the-engineer-manager-pendulum) in an international health-tech company, and I'm really enjoying focusing on technical stuff again. My reading keeps going further back toward origins: Nancy Leveson's work on safety engineering, and complex systems more broadly.

The honest truth is that none of these threads ever ended. I still follow the same people I discovered a decade ago, and I keep adding new ones. Lately I'm exploring LLM-based and agentic systems, while always trying to go back to the origins and learn from the basics.

## What you'll find here

Notes and essays on engineering, career, strategy, reliability, complex systems, and the human side of engineering, written mostly as training tasks for me trying to understand things and share them here, and hopefully useful to you too.
